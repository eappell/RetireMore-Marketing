# The Retirement Expense That Blindsides Everyone
*Medicare isn't free. And if your income is above a certain threshold, it gets a lot less free.*

---

Here's something fun they don't mention at your retirement party: Medicare has a surcharge for people who saved too well. It's called IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount), and it can add hundreds of dollars per month to your premiums based on income you earned two years ago.

Let that sink in. Your Medicare premium in 2026 is based on your 2024 tax return. The IRS and CMS are working together on a two-year time delay, like a financial booby trap you set for yourself without knowing it.

## What Medicare Actually Costs

Most people assume Medicare is free, or close to it. Part A (hospital coverage) usually is, assuming you paid Medicare taxes for at least 10 years. But Part B (doctor visits, outpatient care) and Part D (prescriptions) both have monthly premiums.

In 2025, the standard Part B premium is $185 per month. That's $2,220 per year, per person. If you're a couple, that's $4,440 just for basic outpatient coverage. And that doesn't include supplemental insurance (Medigap), dental, vision, or hearing, none of which Medicare covers well.

Fidelity's annual Retiree Health Care Cost Estimate found that a 65-year-old couple retiring in 2024 should expect to spend approximately $315,000 on healthcare throughout retirement. That number has gone up every single year Fidelity has published this estimate.

## The IRMAA Trap

Now let's talk about the surcharge. If your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) exceeds certain thresholds, your Part B and Part D premiums jump. For 2025, the brackets look roughly like this:

**Single filers:**
- Up to $106,000: standard premium ($185/mo)
- $106,001 to $133,500: $259/mo
- $133,501 to $167,000: $370/mo
- $167,001 to $200,000: $480/mo
- $200,001 to $500,000: $591/mo
- Above $500,000: $628/mo

That top bracket means you're paying $628 per month for Part B alone. That's $7,536 per year, per person. For a couple both in the top bracket, you're looking at over $15,000 a year just in Part B premiums.

And here's the kicker: these thresholds include Roth conversions, capital gains, pension income, and required minimum distributions. So that Roth conversion you did in 2024 to save on future taxes? It might be jacking up your Medicare premiums in 2026.

## The Two-Year Lookback Problem

The two-year lookback creates a planning puzzle that most people don't see coming. You retire at 65, start Medicare, and your premiums are based on your income from age 63, when you were still working full-time and earning your highest salary. So your first year or two of Medicare can be shockingly expensive.

There is a workaround. If you've had a "life-changing event" like retirement, you can file Form SSA-44 to request that Social Security use your current (lower) income instead of the two-year-old number. But you have to know about it and actually file it. Most people don't.

## Long-Term Care: The Other Shoe

Medicare doesn't cover long-term care. This is the part that genuinely catches people off guard. If you need a nursing home, assisted living, or even extended home health care, Medicare covers very little of it.

According to the Genworth Cost of Care Survey, the median annual cost of a semi-private room in a nursing home is over $104,000. A private room is closer to $116,000. And the average length of stay for someone who needs long-term care is about 2.5 years, though many people need it for much longer.

The Department of Health and Human Services estimates that about 70% of people turning 65 today will need some form of long-term care in their remaining years. Seven out of ten. Those aren't small odds.

## What You Can Do About It

First, understand what you're walking into. Most people plan for housing, food, travel, and entertainment in retirement. They forget that healthcare alone could be their single largest expense.

Second, pay attention to your income in the years before and after you turn 65. Strategic Roth conversions, capital gain harvesting, and withdrawal sequencing can help you stay below IRMAA thresholds. But you have to plan this in advance, not react to it after the bill arrives.

Third, have a long-term care conversation. Whether that means insurance, self-funding, or some hybrid approach, don't pretend it won't happen to you. The odds say otherwise.

Healthcare in retirement isn't an afterthought. It's the line item that can quietly consume everything else if you're not paying attention.
