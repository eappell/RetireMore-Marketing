# You Can't Take It With You. But You Can Decide Where It Goes.
*Estate planning, gifting strategies, and the digital afterlife most people forget about entirely.*

---

Nobody enjoys thinking about what happens after they die. It's right up there with colonoscopies and tax audits on the list of important things we avoid until we absolutely can't.

But here's the thing: if you've spent decades building something, whether that's savings, property, relationships, or just a well-organized life, someone is going to have to deal with all of it when you're gone. The question is whether you make that easy for them or leave them to figure it out while grieving.

Most people think estate planning is for the wealthy. It's not. It's for anyone who has stuff, people they care about, and a preference for their family not descending into chaos.

## The Estate Plan You Already Have (Whether You Know It or Not)

If you don't have a will, your state has one for you. It's called intestacy law, and it determines who gets what based on a formula that may or may not reflect your actual wishes.

In most states, if you're married with kids, your spouse gets a portion and your children get the rest. Sounds reasonable until you realize "the rest" might include the house your spouse is living in, and now your children technically own part of it. Awkward Thanksgiving ahead.

According to a 2024 Caring.com survey, only 32% of American adults have a will. Among those aged 55 and older, the number improves to about 46%, but that still means more than half of the people closest to needing one don't have one.

A will is the floor. Not the ceiling. It tells the court what you want. A trust actually makes it happen without the court, which saves your heirs time, money, and the special joy of probate.

## The Gift That Keeps Giving (Tax-Free)

If you have more than you need, and you'd like to see your money do some good while you're alive to enjoy it, the IRS gives you some generous options.

In 2025, you can give up to $18,000 per person, per year, to as many people as you want, with zero tax consequences. Married? You and your spouse can give $36,000 per person, per year. That's a meaningful transfer of wealth, and it happens completely outside of your estate.

Beyond the annual exclusion, there's a lifetime gift and estate tax exemption of $13.61 million per person (2024 number). Most people won't hit that ceiling, but here's the catch: that number is set to drop significantly in 2026 when the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act provisions sunset. The exemption could fall to roughly $6-7 million per person.

If you're in the zone where that matters, the next couple of years are a window. After that, the math changes.

Strategic gifting isn't just about taxes. Researchers at the Indiana University Lilly Family School of Philanthropy have found that donors who give during their lifetime report higher levels of life satisfaction than those who plan to leave everything at death. Seeing your generosity in action, watching a grandchild use the education fund, or helping a cause you care about right now, that provides something a bequest never can.

## The Digital Estate Nobody Plans For

Here's a question: if you died tomorrow, could anyone access your email? Your bank accounts? Your photos? Your social media? Your cryptocurrency?

Most people have somewhere between 50 and 200 online accounts. Passwords, two-factor authentication, recovery codes, security questions. All of it lives in your head (or maybe a password manager that nobody else has access to).

A 2020 study by McAfee estimated that the average American has over 23 accounts that require passwords. Among tech-savvy adults, it's probably double that. When you die, those accounts don't disappear. They sit there, slowly accumulating spam, until someone either figures out the credentials or gives up.

Digital estate planning means documenting your accounts, storing access credentials securely, and designating someone who can manage them. This includes:

- **Financial accounts:** Banks, brokerages, retirement accounts, crypto wallets
- **Email and communication:** The gateway to everything else (password resets go here)
- **Social media:** Do you want your profiles memorialized or deleted?
- **Subscriptions:** Someone needs to cancel Netflix, Spotify, the gym, the meal kits
- **Digital files:** Photos, documents, creative work. Where are they stored and who gets them?

This isn't glamorous. But it's one of the most practical things you can do for the people you leave behind.

## Having the Conversation

The hardest part of legacy planning isn't the paperwork. It's the conversation. Nobody wants to sit their kids down and say, "Here's what happens when I die." But everyone who has done it says the same thing: the relief is enormous, for you and for them.

A 2019 Edward Jones and Age Wave study found that among retirees who had discussed their end-of-life wishes with family, the overwhelming majority reported feeling more at peace and more connected to their loved ones. The conversation itself was the gift.

You don't need to have all the answers. You just need to start. Write down what you have, who you want to have it, and where the important documents are. Tell someone where to find that information. Update it when things change.

Your legacy isn't just your money. It's the order you leave behind, the intentions you make clear, and the burden you lift from the people who love you.
