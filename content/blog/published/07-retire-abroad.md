# What If Your Retirement Dollar Went Twice as Far?
*A growing number of Americans are retiring overseas. Here's what they know that you don't.*

---

There's a version of retirement that most Americans don't seriously consider, even though it solves several of the biggest problems at once: retire somewhere else.

Not somewhere else in the country. Somewhere else on the planet.

Before you dismiss this as a fantasy for trust fund kids and digital nomads, consider some numbers. The average Social Security benefit in 2025 is about $1,900 per month. In most U.S. cities, that barely covers rent. In Lisbon, Portugal, that same $1,900 covers rent, food, healthcare, and a decent social life. In Medellin, Colombia, you'd have money left over.

According to the Social Security Administration, over 700,000 Americans currently receive Social Security payments abroad. That number has been growing steadily for two decades. These aren't outliers. They're people who did the math.

## The Cost of Living Equation

The core appeal is straightforward: your savings go further. International Living's Annual Global Retirement Index consistently ranks countries where a couple can live comfortably on $2,000 to $3,000 per month, including housing. Try doing that in Denver or Charlotte.

Here are some rough monthly budgets for a couple, based on data from Numbeo and various expat surveys:

- **Portugal (Lisbon or Porto):** $2,500-3,500/month
- **Mexico (Lake Chapala, San Miguel):** $1,800-2,800/month
- **Colombia (Medellin):** $1,500-2,500/month
- **Malaysia (Penang):** $1,500-2,200/month
- **Spain (Valencia):** $2,200-3,200/month

Compare those to the U.S. average retirement spending of about $4,345 per month (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2022 Consumer Expenditure Survey). The savings aren't marginal. They're transformative.

## Healthcare Abroad: Better Than You Think

"But what about healthcare?" is always the first question, and it's the right one to ask. The answer might surprise you.

Many popular retirement destinations have universal or semi-universal healthcare systems that are available to residents, often at a fraction of U.S. costs. In Portugal, legal residents can access the national health service (SNS). In Mexico, the IMSS system covers residents for about $500 per year.

Private healthcare in these countries is also dramatically cheaper. A doctor visit in Colombia might cost $15-30 out of pocket. An MRI in Mexico runs $200-400. The same MRI in the U.S. averages $1,325.

The World Health Organization's rankings have consistently placed several popular expat destinations ahead of the United States in overall healthcare system performance. France, Spain, Italy, Portugal, and Costa Rica all rank higher than the U.S. on the WHO's most recent comprehensive assessment.

The caveat: Medicare does not cover you outside the United States (with very limited exceptions). If you retire abroad, you'll need international health insurance or a direct-pay strategy. But even with private international coverage ($200-500/month for comprehensive plans), your total healthcare spend is likely lower than what you'd pay in the U.S. between Medicare premiums, supplemental insurance, and out-of-pocket costs.

## The Visa Question

Every country has different rules for long-term residency. The good news is that many of the most popular retirement destinations have created specific visa programs for retirees, because they want your steady income flowing into their economy.

Portugal's D7 visa requires proof of passive income (Social Security counts). Panama's Pensionado visa is one of the most generous in the world, offering retirees discounts on everything from healthcare to movie tickets. Mexico's temporary and permanent resident visas are straightforward if you can show sufficient income or savings.

The requirements vary, but the common thread is that these countries are actively competing for your retirement dollar. They've made the process accessible because it's in their interest.

## The Things Nobody Mentions

Retiring abroad isn't all cheap wine and ocean views. A few honest considerations:

**Language.** In many countries, you can get by with English, especially in expat communities. But your quality of life improves enormously if you learn the local language. Being unable to communicate with your neighbors, your doctor, or the plumber gets old fast.

**Distance from family.** This is the biggest emotional factor. Cheap flights help, but you're still far away when a grandchild is born or a parent gets sick. Some people solve this by doing half the year abroad, half at home.

**Bureaucracy.** Other countries have bureaucracies too, and dealing with them in a foreign language while navigating unfamiliar systems requires patience. Getting a driver's license, opening a bank account, or setting up utilities can take longer than you're used to.

**Taxes.** U.S. citizens are taxed on worldwide income regardless of where they live. You'll still file with the IRS. However, the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion and various tax treaties can help, and if your primary income is Social Security and investment withdrawals, your tax situation may actually improve.

## Is It Right for You?

Retiring abroad isn't for everyone. But for the right person or couple, it can turn a "just enough" retirement into a "more than enough" one. If your savings are solid but not spectacular, and you're open to adventure, the math might work dramatically better in Lisbon than in Louisville.

At minimum, it's worth running the numbers. You might be surprised at what your existing retirement income can actually buy when you remove the U.S. cost-of-living premium.
