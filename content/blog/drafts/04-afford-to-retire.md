# The 5 Numbers That Tell You If You're Ready to Retire
*Forget the "multiply your salary by 25" rule. Here's what actually matters.*

---

Every financial website has a retirement calculator. Most of them ask you three questions, do some back-of-napkin math, and tell you either "you're fine" or "you're doomed." Neither answer is particularly helpful.

The truth is, retirement readiness isn't one number. It's a handful of numbers that work together. Get them right and you can retire with confidence. Ignore one of them and the whole thing can unravel quietly, usually around year eight, when it's too late to go back to work.

Here are the five numbers that actually matter.

## 1. Your Monthly Nut

This is the most important number and the one most people get wrong. Your "monthly nut" is what it costs you to live, stripped of the expenses that disappear when you stop working (commuting, work clothes, that $14 salad you buy every day because you forgot to pack lunch).

But it also includes expenses that go up in retirement: healthcare (see: Medicare, IRMAA, long-term care), travel, hobbies, and the general reality that you'll be home more and spending more.

A common rule of thumb says you'll need 70-80% of your pre-retirement income. That's not bad as a starting point, but it's also not your number. Your number depends on where you live, whether your house is paid off, your health, and how you plan to spend your time.

Sit down and actually build a budget. Not a fantasy budget. A real one, with line items for property taxes, insurance, groceries, utilities, and the stuff you actually spend money on. Then add 10% for the things you forgot.

## 2. Your Income Floor

Your income floor is the guaranteed money that shows up every month no matter what the stock market does. This includes Social Security, pensions, annuities, and any rental income that's genuinely reliable.

This number matters because it determines how much you need to withdraw from your savings. If your monthly nut is $6,000 and your income floor is $4,000, you only need to generate $2,000 per month from your portfolio. That's a very different situation than needing the full $6,000.

The Employee Benefit Research Institute's 2023 Retirement Confidence Survey found that workers who had calculated how much income they'd need in retirement were significantly more confident and more likely to be on track than those who hadn't. The act of running the numbers, even imperfectly, changes behavior.

## 3. Your Withdrawal Rate

This is the percentage of your savings you pull out each year. The old standard was the "4% rule," which says you can withdraw 4% of your portfolio in year one and adjust for inflation each year after that, with a high probability of not running out of money over 30 years.

The 4% rule comes from a 1994 study by financial planner William Bengen, who tested withdrawal rates against every 30-year period in U.S. market history going back to 1926. He found that 4% survived every single one.

But. The 4% rule assumes a specific portfolio mix (roughly 50/50 stocks and bonds), a 30-year time horizon, and historical U.S. market returns. If you're retiring at 55, you might need 40 years. If bond yields are lower than historical averages (they have been), the safe rate might be closer to 3.5%.

More importantly, the 4% rule is a starting point for thinking, not a final answer. Your withdrawal rate needs to be tested against your specific portfolio, your specific timeline, and a range of market scenarios. This is exactly what Monte Carlo simulations are for.

## 4. Your Tax Drag

Most people think about their retirement savings as one big number. "I have $1.2 million." But not all of that is yours. The IRS has a claim on a good chunk of it, and the size of that claim depends on where the money is sitting.

- **Traditional 401(k)/IRA:** Taxed as ordinary income when you withdraw. If you're in the 22% bracket, that $1.2 million is really more like $936,000.
- **Roth 401(k)/IRA:** Tax-free withdrawals. A dollar here is worth more than a dollar in a traditional account.
- **Taxable brokerage:** You'll owe capital gains tax on the growth, but the basis comes out tax-free.

Your effective tax rate in retirement depends on how much you withdraw, from which accounts, and in what order. Get this wrong and you'll pay tens of thousands more in taxes than you need to.

Research from Vanguard has shown that tax-efficient withdrawal sequencing can add meaningful years to a portfolio's longevity. Drawing from taxable accounts first, then tax-deferred, then Roth is the general rule, but the optimal sequence depends on your specific brackets, RMD requirements, and IRMAA thresholds.

## 5. Your Longevity Buffer

Here's the uncomfortable question: how long do you need your money to last?

Most people plan to age 85 or 90 and call it a day. But the Society of Actuaries has published data showing that for a 65-year-old couple, there's a 45% chance that at least one person will live to 90 and an 18% chance that at least one will live to 95.

Running out of money at 92 is not an abstract risk. It's a real possibility that requires real planning. Your longevity buffer is the gap between when you *expect* to die and when you *might* die. Planning to the expected age isn't enough. You need to plan for the tail.

This is why Monte Carlo simulations matter. They don't just test your plan against average returns. They test it against sequences of bad returns, inflation spikes, and the scenario where you live to 97 and the market crashes twice along the way.

## Putting It All Together

These five numbers interact. A low monthly nut means a lower withdrawal rate. A high income floor means less portfolio risk. A Roth-heavy portfolio means less tax drag. A realistic longevity assumption means you won't run out.

The goal isn't to get one magic number. It's to understand how all five work together in your specific situation. Once you do, the question shifts from "can I afford to retire?" to "what's the best way to do this?"

That's a much better question.
