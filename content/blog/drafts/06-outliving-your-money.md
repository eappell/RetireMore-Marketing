# The Real Risk Nobody Wants to Talk About
*Running out of money at 88 is not a theoretical problem. Here's how to make sure it doesn't happen to you.*

---

There are two kinds of retirement risk that keep financial planners up at night. The first is a market crash right after you retire (that's called "sequence of returns risk" and it's a real thing). The second is simpler and scarier: living longer than your money lasts.

Nobody wants to plan for their own death. But planning for a death that comes too *late*? That's the one that can actually hurt you.

## The Longevity Problem

Americans are living longer than previous generations, and the averages don't tell the full story. The average life expectancy for a 65-year-old man is about 84. For a 65-year-old woman, it's about 87. Those are averages. Half the population lives longer than that.

The Society of Actuaries publishes longevity data that should be posted on every financial advisor's wall. For a married couple both aged 65, there's a 45% chance that at least one of them will live past 90. An 18% chance one of them will see 95. And roughly a 4% chance that someone makes it to 100.

If you planned your retirement to last until 85, and you're still alive at 92, you have a problem that no amount of positive thinking can solve.

## Why the 4% Rule Might Not Save You

The 4% rule was designed for a 30-year retirement. If you retire at 65 and plan to 95, that's 30 years and the rule was built for exactly that scenario. But the rule assumes you withdraw a fixed inflation-adjusted amount every year regardless of market conditions. It doesn't adjust for years when the market drops 30% and you probably should be withdrawing less.

More importantly, the 4% rule was tested against historical U.S. market returns. The U.S. stock market over the past century has been one of the best-performing markets in the history of capitalism. There's no law of nature that says the next 30 years will look like the last 30.

A 2013 study by Wade Pfau and Michael Finke, published in the *Journal of Financial Planning*, found that when using current bond yields and more conservative return assumptions, the safe withdrawal rate drops to around 3%. For someone with a $1 million portfolio, that's the difference between $40,000 a year and $30,000. That's a meaningful lifestyle difference.

## Sequence of Returns: The Silent Killer

It's not just about average returns. It's about when the bad years hit.

Imagine two retirees, both with $1 million, both withdrawing $40,000 per year. Over 30 years, both experience the same average annual return of 7%. But Retiree A gets the bad years early (a crash in years 1-3), while Retiree B gets the bad years late (a crash in years 27-30).

Retiree A runs out of money in year 23. Retiree B dies with $2 million.

Same average return. Completely different outcome. The order matters as much as the average, and you don't get to choose the order.

This is why Monte Carlo simulations exist. Instead of assuming a fixed return every year, a Monte Carlo simulation runs your plan through hundreds or thousands of randomly generated return sequences. It tells you not what will happen, but the probability of different outcomes. "Your plan survives in 87% of scenarios" is infinitely more useful than "your plan works if the market returns 7% every year."

## What Actually Helps

**Diversification across time, not just assets.** Having money in different account types (traditional, Roth, taxable, cash) gives you flexibility to pull from different sources depending on market conditions and tax brackets.

**A cash buffer.** Keeping 1-2 years of living expenses in cash or short-term bonds means you don't have to sell stocks during a downturn. This dramatically reduces sequence-of-returns risk.

**Flexible spending.** People who can reduce spending by 10-15% during bad market years have dramatically higher success rates in Monte Carlo simulations. Rigid spending is the enemy of a long retirement.

**Delaying Social Security.** Every year you delay past 62 increases your benefit by 6-8%. That higher guaranteed income floor reduces how much you need to withdraw from your portfolio, which extends its life.

## The Conversation Nobody Has

Most retirement planning tools stop at "here's your projected balance at age 85." That's useful, but it's incomplete. What happens at 86? At 90? At 95?

The plans that actually work are the ones that account for the full range of possibilities. Not just the most likely scenario, but the scenarios in the tails. Because if you're the one who lives to 97, the most likely scenario is irrelevant. Your scenario is the one that matters.

Run the simulation. See the probabilities. Then build a plan that works even when things don't go according to plan. That's what longevity planning actually means.
