# The $100K Social Security Mistake Most People Make
*Your claiming age isn't a minor detail. It's the biggest financial decision of your entire retirement.*

---

You've been paying into Social Security for decades. You've seen the line items on your paycheck. You've maybe even logged into ssa.gov once or twice to peek at your estimated benefit. And at some point, you probably thought: "I'll just start collecting at 62. Why leave money on the table?"

Here's the problem with that logic: you're not leaving money on the table by waiting. You're leaving it on the table by *not* waiting.

## The Math That Changes Everything

Social Security lets you start collecting as early as 62 or as late as 70. Most people know this. What most people don't fully appreciate is how dramatically the numbers shift depending on when you file.

If your full retirement age (FRA) is 67 and your full benefit is $2,500 a month, here's what happens:

- **Claim at 62:** $1,750/month (30% reduction, permanent)
- **Claim at 67:** $2,500/month (your full benefit)
- **Claim at 70:** $3,100/month (24% increase, permanent)

That's a difference of $1,350 per month between claiming at 62 and claiming at 70. Over a 20-year retirement, that's $324,000. Over 25 years, it's north of $400,000.

And those numbers don't even account for cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs), which compound on a higher base if you delay.

## "But What If I Die Early?"

This is the most common pushback, and it's not unreasonable. If you claim at 70 but die at 72, you collected for two years instead of the ten you would have gotten by starting at 62. That's a real risk.

But here's what the data actually says. According to the Social Security Administration's actuarial life tables, a 62-year-old man today has a 50% chance of living past 83. A 62-year-old woman has a 50% chance of living past 86. And if you're married, there's a 50% chance that at least one of you will live past 90.

The break-even point for delaying from 62 to 70 is typically around age 80 to 82. After that, every month is pure upside. And for most people reading this, the odds of reaching 82 are better than a coin flip.

A 2019 study from the National Bureau of Economic Research found that the vast majority of retirees would benefit from delaying Social Security, yet most claim early. The researchers estimated that early claiming costs the median household approximately $182,370 in lifetime wealth. Not because people did the math wrong, but because they never ran the math at all.

## The Spousal Strategy Most Couples Miss

If you're married, this gets even more interesting. Social Security has spousal and survivor benefits that can significantly change the calculus.

When one spouse dies, the surviving spouse gets the higher of the two benefits. So if you're the higher earner and you claimed at 62, your spouse inherits your reduced benefit. But if you delayed to 70, your spouse inherits a much larger check for the rest of their life.

For couples, delaying the higher earner's benefit isn't just about your own retirement. It's a form of life insurance for your partner. The Center for Retirement Research at Boston College has published extensively on this, noting that optimal claiming strategies for couples can differ by tens of thousands of dollars compared to the "just claim when you can" approach.

## Why Most People Still Claim Early

If the math is so clear, why do 34% of men and 39% of women still claim at 62?

A few reasons. Some people genuinely need the income. That's fair. But for many, it comes down to three things: fear that the program will be cut (it won't disappear, though benefits could be reduced), a desire to "get their money back" after decades of paying in, and plain old impatience.

There's also a psychological factor. Behavioral economists call it "present bias." A dollar today feels more real than $1.24 eight years from now, even when the math says otherwise.

## What You Should Actually Do

Before you file, run your specific numbers. Not a rough estimate, not a "my neighbor said" calculation. Your actual numbers, with your actual earnings history, your actual life expectancy assumptions, and (if applicable) your spouse's situation.

The difference between the best and worst claiming strategy for your household could easily be six figures. That's not a rounding error. That's a decade of grocery bills.

Take 10 minutes. Run the scenarios. Then decide.
