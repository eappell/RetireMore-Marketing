# The Tax Loophole That Closes the Day You Turn 73
*Why the years between retirement and RMDs are the most valuable tax planning window of your life.*

---

There's a window of time in most people's retirement that's worth a small fortune in tax savings. It opens the day you stop working and it closes the year you turn 73 (or 75, depending on when you were born). Most people sail right through it without realizing what they missed.

It's called the Roth conversion window, and if you have a traditional 401(k) or IRA, you need to understand it.

## The Setup

When you're working, your income is (hopefully) at its highest. Your tax bracket reflects that. Every dollar in your traditional 401(k) or IRA was contributed pre-tax, meaning you got a deduction when it went in. The deal with the IRS is simple: you don't pay tax now, but you'll pay tax later, when you withdraw.

Most people assume "later" means retirement, when their income will be lower and their tax rate will drop. And for many people, that's true. But here's the twist.

When you retire but before you start Social Security and before required minimum distributions (RMDs) kick in, your taxable income can drop dramatically. Maybe you're living off savings or a spouse's income. Maybe you have a small pension but nothing like your working salary.

During those years, your federal tax bracket might be 10% or 12%. Compare that to the 22% or 24% bracket you were in while working, or the 22%+ bracket you'll be in once Social Security and RMDs stack up in your 70s.

That gap is the window. And Roth conversions are how you exploit it.

## How Roth Conversions Work

A Roth conversion is simple in concept: you move money from a traditional IRA or 401(k) into a Roth IRA. You pay income tax on the amount you convert in the year you do it. After that, the money grows tax-free and comes out tax-free, forever.

The strategy is to convert in years when your tax rate is low, so you pay 10-12% tax now instead of 22-24% later. If you convert $50,000 in a year when you're in the 12% bracket, you pay $6,000 in tax. If you waited and that $50,000 came out as an RMD in the 22% bracket, you'd pay $11,000. That's $5,000 saved on a single year's conversion.

Do that for five or six years in a row and the savings compound dramatically.

## The RMD Cliff

Required minimum distributions start at age 73 for people born between 1951 and 1959, and at age 75 for those born in 1960 or later (thanks to the SECURE 2.0 Act). Once RMDs begin, the IRS forces you to withdraw a percentage of your traditional retirement accounts every year, whether you need the money or not.

The percentage starts around 3.8% and increases with age. On a $1 million traditional IRA, that's $38,000 in your first RMD year. By age 80, it's closer to $47,000. By 85, about $55,000.

Stack those RMDs on top of Social Security, and suddenly you're back in a higher tax bracket. Worse, that combined income can trigger IRMAA surcharges on your Medicare premiums. So you're paying more in both income tax and healthcare costs.

A study by Vanguard found that strategic Roth conversions during low-income years can reduce total lifetime tax liability by 10-20% for many retirees. The key word is "strategic." Converting too much in one year can push you into a higher bracket and negate the benefit. The goal is to fill up the lower brackets each year without spilling over.

## The IRMAA Connection

Here's where it gets sneaky. Roth conversions count as taxable income in the year you do them. If you convert too aggressively, you can trigger IRMAA surcharges on your Medicare premiums two years later (remember the lookback rule).

So the optimal strategy is a balancing act: convert enough to fill the 12% or 22% bracket, but not so much that you cross an IRMAA threshold. This requires knowing exactly where the brackets and IRMAA tiers fall, and modeling your income year by year.

It's not impossibly complex, but it's not something you can do on the back of a napkin either.

## The Window Is Smaller Than You Think

Let's say you retire at 62 and RMDs start at 73. That's an 11-year window. But Social Security usually starts somewhere in there (most people claim between 62 and 67), which raises your taxable income and narrows the effective window.

If you claim Social Security at 65 and your benefit is $2,500 per month, that's $30,000 in annual income (up to 85% of which is taxable). Now your conversion space in the 12% bracket just shrank significantly.

The real sweet spot is often the 2-5 years between retirement and Social Security. Those are the years when your taxable income is lowest and your conversion opportunity is greatest. Miss them and you're looking at higher rates for every dollar you convert.

## What to Do

Map out your income year by year from now through age 75. Identify the years when your taxable income drops. Model how much you can convert in each of those years while staying in a favorable bracket and below IRMAA thresholds.

Then actually do the conversions. Every year you skip is a year of low-bracket conversion space you can never get back.

The window closes. The tax bill doesn't.
