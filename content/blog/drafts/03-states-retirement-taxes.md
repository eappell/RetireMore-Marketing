# Where You Retire Could Save You $10,000 a Year
*A state-by-state breakdown of how retirement income gets taxed, and why your zip code matters more than you think.*

---

You've probably spent more time picking a restaurant for Friday night than thinking about how your state taxes your retirement income. That's understandable. State tax policy isn't exactly dinner conversation material.

But here's the thing: the difference between retiring in New Jersey and retiring in Florida could be $8,000 to $15,000 per year in state taxes alone. Over a 25-year retirement, that's potentially $200,000 to $375,000. For that kind of money, you might at least want to look at the menu.

## The States That Don't Tax Retirement Income

Let's start with the easy wins. Nine states have no state income tax at all:

Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming.

If you live in one of these states, congratulations. Your Social Security, pension, 401(k) withdrawals, and IRA distributions are all state-tax-free. You can stop reading this section and skip ahead.

For everyone else, it gets complicated. Because "no income tax" isn't the only way a state can be retirement-friendly. Some states have an income tax but exempt retirement income from it. Others tax some retirement income but not all of it. And a few states tax everything, including Social Security, which feels personally offensive.

## Social Security: Who Taxes It?

As of 2025, only nine states tax Social Security benefits. If you live in one of them, you're essentially paying state tax on income you already paid federal tax on, which was already taxed when you earned it. Triple taxation, more or less.

The states that still tax Social Security (with some exemptions and phase-outs): Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Montana, New Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah, Vermont, and West Virginia.

Most of these have income thresholds below which Social Security is exempt. But if your retirement income is above those thresholds (and if you've saved well, it probably is), you'll be paying.

## The Property Tax Wildcard

Here's where people get tripped up. A state with no income tax can still be expensive if property taxes are high. Texas has no income tax, but the average effective property tax rate is 1.6%, among the highest in the country. On a $400,000 home, that's $6,400 per year.

Compare that to South Carolina, which has a modest income tax but offers a $50,000 homestead exemption for residents over 65 and some of the lowest property tax rates in the country.

The Tax Foundation publishes annual data on combined state and local tax burdens. Their analysis consistently shows that the total tax picture varies dramatically even among states that look similar on paper. A "low tax" state for a working professional might not be a low tax state for a retiree, and vice versa.

## Real Examples That Might Surprise You

Let's say you're a couple with $80,000 in combined retirement income (Social Security, pension, 401(k) withdrawals).

In **California**, you'd pay roughly $2,800 in state income tax. California doesn't tax Social Security, but it taxes everything else at normal rates.

In **Pennsylvania**, you'd pay $0 in state income tax on that same income. Pennsylvania doesn't tax Social Security, pensions, or retirement account withdrawals.

In **New York**, you'd pay around $3,000, though the first $20,000 of pension/retirement income is exempt.

In **Florida**, obviously $0. No income tax.

Same income. Same retirement. Thousands of dollars in difference, every single year.

## It's Not Just About Taxes

Taxes are the most quantifiable factor, but they're not the only one. Cost of living, healthcare access, proximity to family, climate, and quality of life all matter. Nobody should move to a state they hate just to save on taxes. (Though plenty of people have moved to states they love and gotten a tax break as a bonus.)

A 2021 study by United Van Lines found that among Americans aged 55 and older, the top reasons for relocating were lifestyle and retirement, with tax considerations ranking as a secondary but meaningful factor. People move for the life they want. The tax savings are the cherry on top.

The point isn't that you need to uproot everything. The point is that if you're already thinking about where you want to spend the next chapter, the tax implications deserve more than a passing glance. A couple hundred dollars a month in tax savings adds up to real money over a long retirement.

## What to Do Next

Look up how your current state treats Social Security, pension income, 401(k)/IRA withdrawals, and property. Then compare it to the states you've been daydreaming about.

You might find that the beach town you've been eyeing is also $8,000 a year cheaper. Or you might find that staying put is the right call, but at least you'll know what it's costing you.
